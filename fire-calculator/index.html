Your investments

FIRE calculator for India

FIRE stands for Financial Independence, Retire Early: having enough saved and invested that it can pay your living costs for the rest of your life, so working becomes a choice, not a need. The amount you need is your FIRE number. This calculator works out your number and the age you'll reach it, with EPF and NPS unlocking at 58 and 60, and Lean, Fat and Coast FIRE.

Lean, regular and Fat FIRE

Different lifestyles after you stop working. Lean FIRE is a frugal retirement (70% of your spending today); Fat FIRE is a comfortable one with room for travel and extras (150%). Your FIRE uses your spending as it is. Each is shown with the same savings. Read: Lean, Fat and Coast FIRE

Same savings, different finish lines. Lean FIRE: stopping work on a tighter budget, 70% of what you spend today. Fat FIRE: a more comfortable life, 150%. The ages show when each becomes possible.

How the calculator works

  • Saving: your savings, EPF and NPS contributions go in every month and rise by the percentage you set each year, earning their returns.
  • FI age: the first age at which your investments outside EPF and NPS can pay your spending (rising with inflation) every month until your plan-until age, with EPF and NPS added when they unlock. Withdrawals use our shared systematic withdrawal (SWP) engine, month by month.
  • EPF: keeps earning until 58, or comes out a year after you stop working if you choose (EPF Scheme 2026 allows final settlement after 12 months out of work).
  • NPS: stays invested until 60; then up to 80% as a lump sum and the rest buys a pension at 6.5% a year (rules from our NPS calculator).
  • FIRE number: what you need at FI: the smallest pot outside EPF and NPS that lasts, plus your EPF and NPS then. Lean and Fat FIRE use 70% and 150% of your spending. Coast FIRE is worked out for stopping at 60.
  • Everything is before tax, so use returns after tax and costs.

Sources

Frequently asked questions

What is FIRE?

Financial Independence, Retire Early: saving and investing enough that your investments can pay your living costs for the rest of your life, so working becomes optional. Your FIRE number is the amount you need at that point.

Is 25 times my yearly expenses enough in India?

Often not, if you retire early. The 25× rule comes from US data with lower inflation. With 6% inflation in India and 40 or more years to fund, the calculator usually finds 27× to 35×. It works out your own number from your spending, inflation and returns, year by year, instead of using a fixed multiple.

Why does the calculator treat EPF and NPS separately?

Because you can't spend them right away. NPS stays invested until 60, when up to 80% can be taken as a lump sum and the rest buys a pension. EPF keeps earning interest until 58 if you leave it; you can also take it out 12 months after you stop working (2 months under a change approved in November 2026, once notified). Until then, your other investments have to pay the bills: that's the bridge.

Should I take my EPF out early?

You can, a year after you stop working, but you lose the 8.25% it earns (tax-free on contributions within the limits). The calculator lets you choose; taking it early rarely helps unless your other investments are short.

What are Lean, Fat and Coast FIRE?

Lean FIRE: retiring on a smaller budget (the calculator uses 70% of your spending). Fat FIRE: a more comfortable one (150%). Coast FIRE: having enough invested already that, with no more saving, it grows to what you need by 60, so you only have to earn your current expenses.

What returns should I use?

Returns after tax and costs. Before FI, a mix of equity funds and EPF might average 10–11%; after FI, most people move part of their money to safer options, so 7–8% is more realistic. Try a percentage point lower to see how much margin you have.

Is the savings rate on gross or take-home pay?

Take-home. The calculator takes your take-home as what you spend plus what you save each month (EPF and NPS contributions are counted separately, since they come out before take-home).

What about health insurance and big one-off costs?

Budget for them in your monthly spending, or add a separate fund. Early retirees lose employer health cover, so a family floater with a top-up is essential; premiums rise with age.
