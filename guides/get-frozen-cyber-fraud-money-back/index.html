Lost money to a cyber fraud? How to get frozen money back

You called 1930 in time and the police froze part of the money in the fraudster's account. That's the hard part done, but the money doesn't come back on its own. Here are the three ways to get it released to you, the papers you need, and why you shouldn't wait: the next National Lok Adalat sits on 12 December.

The short answer

A freeze only holds the money; you have to ask for it back. There are three routes. 1. The Money Restoration Module on the cybercrime portal: online, and for up to ₹50,000 frozen in one account, no FIR or court order is needed. 2. The National Lok Adalat on 12 December: free, through the investigating officer and your District Legal Services Authority. 3. An application to the magistrate under section 503 of the BNSS. Don't sit on it: under the Home Ministry's procedure, a bank must lift a hold after 90 days if no court or restoration order has come.

Frozen isn't the same as returned

  • What 1930 does: your complaint goes to the banks and payment apps the money passed through, and they put a hold on whatever is still there.
  • What it doesn't do: send it back. The money sits in the fraudster's (often a rented "mule") account until someone with authority orders its release to you.
  • The clock: the Home Ministry's procedure for cyber fraud complaints, issued on 2 January 2026, tells banks to lift a hold within 90 days if no court or restoration order is in place. Start a route below well before that.
  • Only frozen money can come back this way. Anything already withdrawn or moved on is a matter for the police investigation.

Meena's ₹85,000

Made-up example. Meena, 31, in Pune, paid ₹85,000 to a fake "courier customs" caller over three UPI transfers. She called 1930 within an hour. The police later told her ₹60,000 was frozen: ₹35,000 in one account and ₹25,000 in another. The other ₹25,000 had already been withdrawn.

Route 1: Money Restoration Module

  • What it is: an online request on the National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal (cybercrime.gov.in) to restore money frozen in the fraudster's account, without visiting a police station, bank or court.
  • You need: the 14-digit acknowledgement number from your 1930 call or portal complaint, and money that is still frozen.
  • Up to ₹50,000 frozen in one account: no FIR or court order; the refund goes through on a police report and an indemnity bond you sign online.
  • More than ₹50,000 in one account: an FIR has to be registered first.
  • Where it works: it has been rolled out state by state, starting with states such as Telangana and Uttar Pradesh. If you can't use it, ask your district cyber cell.
  • Beware of "agents" who offer to speed up a refund for a fee. The process is free, and that offer is often a second scam.

Route 2: National Lok Adalat, 12 December

  • What it is: a one-day sitting of courts across India, held four times a year. The 2026 dates are 14 March, 9 May, 12 September and 12 December.
  • Why it helps: banks, cyber police and judicial officers sit together and many frozen-money claims are settled on the day. In Telangana's December 2024 sitting, police helped 4,893 cyber fraud victims get ₹33.2 crore back this way.
  • How to get listed: ask the investigating officer on your complaint whether the money is frozen, how much, and to put your case up for 12 December. Also contact your District Legal Services Authority (at the district court); a case can be taken up before it ever reaches a court.
  • Cost: no court fee and no lawyer needed.
  • Before you agree: an award has the force of a court decree, and there's no appeal. If only part of your money is frozen, check that settling won't close your claim to the rest.

Missed the December listing? The next National Lok Adalat will be in 2027; ask the DLSA for the date, and use route 1 or 3 meanwhile.

Route 3: an order from the magistrate

  • What it is: an application to the magistrate where the case is registered, asking for the frozen money to be released to you under section 503 (or 497) of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (sections 457 and 451 of the old CrPC).
  • How it works: the magistrate calls for a police report; once the order is made, you usually sign a bond or undertaking, and the bank releases the money.
  • When to use it: larger amounts, when the module isn't available, or when you can't wait for a Lok Adalat. A free legal aid lawyer from the DLSA can help with the application.

Papers to keep ready

  1. Complaint number from 1930 or cybercrime.gov.in, and the FIR number if there is one.
  2. Your bank statement showing each debit, with UTR or transaction IDs.
  3. Details of the receiving accounts (UPI IDs, account numbers), from the complaint or the police.
  4. The police's note of how much is frozen, and in which accounts.
  5. ID and address proof, and a cancelled cheque or account details for the refund.
  6. The indemnity bond, signed when asked: it promises to return the money if a court later decides it wasn't yours.

If the money was already withdrawn

  • Unauthorised debit? If you didn't approve the transaction (no OTP or PIN shared), tell your bank in writing at once. Under RBI's customer protection rules, reporting within three working days can mean you bear no loss.
  • You approved it under a trick? The bank isn't usually liable, but complain to it anyway; if there's no reply within 30 days, or you're unhappy with it, you can go to the RBI Ombudsman at cms.rbi.org.in within 90 days.
  • Keep following the investigation: money traced later can still be frozen and released.

How these scams start: the digital arrest scam, wedding invite APKs, WhatsApp Web takeovers, and the RBI's proposed stop switch and trusted person. More in Stay safe.
