Digital arrest scam: how it works and what to do if you get the call

A video call from someone in a police uniform says your Aadhaar is linked to a drugs parcel or money laundering, and you're under “digital arrest” until you clear your name. It's a scam, and it has taken thousands of crores from Indians, many of them educated and careful. Here's how it works and exactly what to do.

The short answer

There is no such thing as a "digital arrest" in Indian law. No police, CBI, ED, customs, RBI, TRAI or court officer arrests or questions you on a video call, keeps you on camera, or asks you to move money to "verify" it. If you get such a call, hang up, tell someone you trust, and report the number. If you've already sent money, call 1930 at once and report at cybercrime.gov.in: the faster you report, the better the chance your bank can stop the money.

Lost money? Act now: call 1930 (national cyber fraud helpline) and file at cybercrime.gov.in. Got a suspicious call or message but paid nothing? Report it on Chakshu (Sanchar Saathi).

How the scam works

Police and the Home Ministry's Indian Cyber Crime Coordination Centre (I4C) describe the same pattern again and again. A typical call goes like this:

  1. The hook: a call, often a recorded voice, says a courier parcel in your name holds drugs or fake passports, or your mobile number or Aadhaar has been used for crime and will be blocked.
  2. The handover: you're "connected" to a police, CBI, ED or narcotics "officer", then moved to a WhatsApp or Skype video call.
  3. The show: a uniform, a police-station backdrop, official-looking logos, and an "arrest warrant" or court order sent on WhatsApp.
  4. The isolation: you're told the case is secret, not to disconnect, not to tell family, and to stay on camera, sometimes for hours or days.
  5. The money: to "prove your innocence" you must move your savings to a "safe" or "RBI verification" account, often by breaking FDs, selling mutual funds or taking a loan, with a promise that it will be refunded.

It works because it uses fear, authority and urgency to stop you thinking clearly, and cuts you off from anyone who could tell you it's a scam.

Cases fell sharply in 2025 after awareness campaigns and the blocking of thousands of WhatsApp and Skype accounts, but single losses are still huge. In January 2026 a retired doctor couple in south Delhi lost ₹14.85 crore after being kept on video calls for over two weeks; the money went to eight accounts and was spread across hundreds of mule accounts. The Supreme Court took up digital arrest cases on its own in October 2025 and on 1 December 2025 made the CBI the main agency to investigate them across India.

Red flags

  • Any "officer" who wants to question or arrest you on a video call.
  • Being told not to disconnect or not to tell your family.
  • Documents, warrants or ID cards sent on WhatsApp.
  • Any request to transfer money, break an FD, redeem investments or take a loan "for verification".
  • Calls that start with a recorded message about a parcel, your SIM or your Aadhaar.

If you get the call

The government's advice, in three words: ruko, socho, action lo (stop, think, act).

  1. Hang up. You are not under arrest and you don't need anyone's permission to disconnect.
  2. Talk to someone you trust, right away. The scam depends on keeping you alone.
  3. Share nothing: no OTP, Aadhaar, PAN, bank details or screen-sharing.
  4. Check for yourself by calling your local police station on a number you look up, not one the caller gives you.
  5. Report the number on Chakshu at sancharsaathi.gov.in within 30 days, so it can be blocked.

If you've already paid

  1. Call 1930 immediately. The national helpline feeds a system that alerts banks to hold the money; it had saved over ₹4,386 crore across more than 13 lakh complaints by early 2025. Minutes matter.
  2. File a complaint at cybercrime.gov.in, and note the acknowledgement number.
  3. Call your bank's fraud line to block further debits and ask it to flag the receiving accounts.
  4. Keep everything: numbers, screenshots, the "documents" you were sent, and every transaction ID.
  5. Follow up with your local police or cyber cell, which turns the complaint into an FIR.

Because you made the transfers yourself, the bank rules that protect you from unauthorised transactions usually don't apply. That's why speed of reporting matters so much.

Protect your savings before it happens

  • Set limits: lower the daily UPI and net-banking transfer limits in your bank app to what you actually need. Large transfers then need a deliberate step.
  • Keep spending money separate: a small account for UPI and cards, with savings and FDs elsewhere.
  • Agree a family rule: no large transfer, FD break or loan without first talking to one named person.
  • Talk to your parents: people over 60 are targeted most. Show them this page, and make sure they know about 1930.
  • Keep an emergency fund so a shock doesn't push you into a panicked decision: see the budget planner.

Already lost money, and some of it was frozen? It won’t come back on its own: how to get frozen money back, including the 12 December Lok Adalat.
