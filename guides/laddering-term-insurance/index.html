Should you split your term insurance into two policies?

Your family needs the most cover while loans are big and children are young. Laddering means buying two policies that end at different ages, so you stop paying for cover you no longer need. Here's when that saves money, and when it leaves a gap.

By Salarist

Published 27 Nov 2026Term insurance7 min read

The short answer

Instead of one ₹2.75 Cr policy to 60, the calculator's example family could buy ₹2 Cr to 60 plus ₹75 L to 50, when their home loan ends. On indicative premiums, that saves about ₹64,640 over 28 years (₹9,843 in today's money), and the first year actually costs ₹670 more. The split only works when the longer policy is the bigger one. Flip it, with the big policy ending at 50, and the family is ₹1.23 Cr short of cover from 50.

The need rises at first with inflation, peaks at 47, then falls as the loan is repaid, goals are met and fewer years of support are left. The policy to 60 is sized to the highest need from the split age onwards; the short policy tops it up to the peak until then. "Crore-years" is cover multiplied by years. It shows how much less cover you pay for, but not what that costs, because premiums don't rise in a straight line with either.

Three things stand out:

  • The saving is small. A 10% cut in cover-years becomes about a 7% cut in premiums, because…
  • Bigger covers cost less per lakh. ₹2 crore costs well under twice ₹1 crore, so two smaller policies lose part of that discount. That's why every split costs more in the first year.
  • Splitting late barely helps. At 55 the short policy has to be ₹1 Cr, and the saving almost disappears.

A big policy that ends early?

A common version of this idea is the other way round: a large policy A to 45 or 50 for the years when parents, spouse and children all depend on you, and a smaller policy B to 60 or beyond, mainly for your spouse. A's premium stops early, and B is cheap because it's small.

For this family, that's ₹2 Cr to 50 + ₹75 L to 60. It is the cheapest plan in the table at ₹6.4 L, but only because it covers much less. From 50 the family needs ₹1.98 Cr and has ₹75 L: ₹1.23 Cr short, and still ₹83.9 L short at 55.

This shape works only when most of the need really ends with policy A: for example, if the cover is mainly for a big loan or the children's education, and your spouse earns or your savings would carry the household after that. Run the calculator with your numbers and look at the need at the age A would end. If B covers that, the flipped split is fine. If it doesn't, the bigger policy has to be the longer one.

Cover past 60 for your spouse

Running a policy to 70 or 75 costs a lot more each year: in one insurer's published table, about 28% more to 70 and 44% more to 75 than to 60, for the same cover. For this family:

  • Taking policy B in the flipped plan to 70 instead of 60 raises its total from ₹3.3 L to ₹5.7 L, and the ₹1.23 Cr gap at 50 is still there.
  • Taking the whole ₹2.75 Cr to 70 costs ₹15.7 L in total against ₹9 L to 60. The split at 50, with the ₹2 Cr policy to 70, costs ₹13.4 L.

By 59 this family's need is down to ₹12.3 L, because support was planned to 60. Cover past 60 is worth it only if your spouse would have to live on it in retirement, which is the case when your retirement savings aren't yet enough to support them. Read Term cover until 60 or 70?

If the aim is that the money goes only to your spouse and children, you don't need a separate policy for that. Name them as nominees, or buy the policy under section 6 of the Married Women's Property Act, 1874, which you choose on the proposal form. The payout is then held in trust for your wife and children: it doesn't form part of your estate, and your creditors can't claim it.

Is it worth it?

  • Yes, when a large loan or another big need ends well before your support years do, and the long policy still covers what's left.
  • Less so for smaller covers, or when the split is late: the per-lakh discount on one large policy eats most of the saving.
  • Never size the policies from the premium. Size them from the need at each age, then compare the cost of one policy and two.
  • Buy both at once: a second policy bought later costs more, needs fresh medical checks, and may be refused if your health changes. Tell each insurer about the other policy.
  • Alternatives: some plans let cover fall in steps, or let you reduce it later. Compare their premiums with two separate policies.

How these numbers were worked out

Cover needed is from the term insurance calculator's own code, with its default family (age 32, ₹50,000 a month expenses, 28 years of support, ₹40 lakh of loans over 18 years, education and other goals). Each policy is rounded up as the calculator does.

Premiums are indicative, built from insurers' published tables as quoted by Ditto Insurance, not quotes. The starting point is ₹1 crore to 60 for a healthy non-smoking man: ICICI Prudential iProtect Smart Plus at ₹12,683 a year at 30 and ₹16,382 at 35, which gives ₹14,163 at 32 on a straight line. That is adjusted for cover size using HDFC Life Click 2 Protect Supreme at age 32 (₹50 lakh ₹13,595, ₹1 crore ₹20,950, ₹2 crore ₹36,359, ₹3 crore ₹51,482), and for end age using Axis Max Life Smart Term Plan Plus (to 70 is 27.9% more than to 60, to 75 44.3% more). Cover to 50 is taken as costing 1/1.4 of cover to 60, from Tata AIA's note that cover to 60 costs about 40% more than to 50. End ages 48 and 55 are on the straight line through 50 and 60. Premiums stay level for the whole term. Read how we check our numbers.
